# Creativity Framework Protocols

Load this file when deploying a creativity framework during a brainstorm session.
Run frameworks **silently** — ask through them, don't announce or explain them.

---

## First Principles

**When**: User is stuck in conventional thinking, "that's how it's always done", or needs to question inherited assumptions.

Questions to ask:
- "Let's strip away everything that exists. What do you actually *know* is true about this problem — not assumed, not inherited, just true?"
- "If you had to rebuild [X] from scratch with no constraints, what would you keep?"
- "What's the actual job that [thing] is doing? Not the form — the job."

---

## HMW — How Might We

**When**: Problem is too narrowly framed, user is solution-anchored, or ideation needs a better launch point.

Reframe their problem into a HMW statement before ideating:

> "Before we generate ideas — let me try rephrasing this. 'How might we [action] for [user] so that [outcome]?' Does that framing feel right, or is it off?"

Formula: `How might we [action verb] for [user] so that [desired outcome]?`

Why it works: "How *might* we" admits uncertainty and opens possibility space vs. "How *will* we" which anchors to one answer.

---

## SCAMPER

**When**: User has an existing idea or concept to expand, stress-test, or make more creative. Pick 2–3 lenses, not all 7.

- **Substitute** — "What if you replaced [X] with something completely different?"
- **Combine** — "What would happen if you merged this with [unexpected thing]?"
- **Adapt** — "What's a completely different domain that solves a similar problem? How do they do it?"
- **Modify / Magnify** — "What if you made [this element] 10x bigger / faster / more extreme?"
- **Put to other use** — "Who else could use this? What other problem does it solve?"
- **Eliminate** — "What's the most important thing you could remove? What survives?"
- **Reverse** — "What if you did this completely backwards?"

## Reverse Brainstorm

**When**: User is blocked, ideas feel safe and predictable, or session needs an energy reset.

Step 1 — ask the inverse:
> "Let's flip this. How would you *guarantee* this fails? What's the worst version of [X] you could possibly build?"

Step 2 — after they answer, flip each failure mode:
> "Now take each of those — what's the opposite of each failure mode?"

Why it works: Removing the pressure of "come up with good ideas" unlocks fluency. Reversal transforms negatives into design principles.

## Six Thinking Hats

**When**: User is stuck in one mental mode (usually critical or logical), a decision needs multiple angles, or the evaluation phase begins. Deploy 2–3 hats, not all six.

- **White** (Facts): "Setting aside opinions — what do we actually *know* here? What data exists?"
- **Red** (Emotion): "What does your gut say? Not the analysis — the feeling."
- **Black** (Risk): "What's the worst realistic outcome? What could genuinely go wrong?"
- **Yellow** (Optimism): "Assume this works perfectly — what does success look like?"
- **Green** (Creative): "Forget what's realistic. What's a wild idea you haven't said out loud yet?"
- **Blue** (Process): "What's missing from how we're thinking about this? What question haven't we asked?"

## Analogical Thinking

**When**: User needs fresh inspiration, ideas feel derivative, or they're too close to the problem to see it differently.

Questions to ask:
- "What's a completely different domain — not [their industry] — that faces the same underlying challenge? How do they solve it?"
- "If [someone from an unexpected field] had this problem, what would they do?"
- "Nature solves [similar challenge] through [analogy] — does that pattern suggest anything here?"

Why it works: Cross-domain analogies bypass fixedness — the mind can't default to "how we always do it" when the domain is unrecognizable.

---

## JTBD — Jobs To Be Done

**When**: User is ideating around a product or service and thinking in features, not user value.

Questions to ask:
- "Forget the product for a second. What job is the user trying to get done? What progress are they trying to make?"
- "What do they use *right now* when this product doesn't exist? Why would they 'fire' it?"
- "How does using [product] make them *feel*? How does it make them look to others?"

Three dimensions to probe:
- **Functional**: What task are they completing?
- **Social**: How does it affect how others see them?
- **Emotional**: How does it make them feel about themselves?

## Pre-mortem

**When**: User is about to commit to a decision or plan — especially a high-stakes one. Use *before* committing, not after. More powerful than risk-listing because it assumes failure has already happened.

Step 1 — set the scene:
> "Let's say 6 months from now, [this project / decision] has completely failed. Not 'might fail' — it *has* failed. What happened?"

Step 2 — dig into each failure cause:
> "That one — is it something you *know* could happen, or something you've been *assuming* won't?"

Step 3 — flip to mitigation:
> "For each of those causes, is there anything you could do *right now* to reduce the probability?"

Why it works: Prospective hindsight — imagining failure has *already* occurred increases the ability to identify causes by ~30% vs. asking "what could go wrong?" (Gary Klein, 1998).

## Five Whys

**When**: The same problem keeps recurring, the user is treating symptoms not root cause, or "why" hasn't been asked deeply enough yet.

Run iteratively — each answer becomes the next question:
> "Why does [problem] happen?"
> "Why does [that answer] happen?"
> *(repeat until the root cause surfaces — usually 3–5 levels deep)*

Stop when the answer points to a **system, habit, or assumption** rather than an event. That's the root.

Caution: Works best on single causal chains. Avoid when the problem has multiple independent causes.

---

## Second-order Thinking

**When**: User is focused on immediate outcomes and missing downstream consequences. Especially useful for decisions with long-term ripple effects (career, product strategy, team structure).

Core question sequence:
> "If you do X, what happens immediately?"
> "And then — what happens as a result of *that*? Who else is affected?"
> "If *everyone* in a similar situation also did X, what happens?"

Push at least two levels deep:
- **First order**: direct, obvious consequence
- **Second order**: what happens as a result of that
- **Third order** (if needed): what happens as a result of that

Why it works: Most people stop at first-order. Second-order thinking is where competitive advantage and avoided mistakes live.

---

## Rapid Ideation

**When**: User is overthinking each idea, generating too slowly, or needs to break perfectionism in the diverge phase.

Run it as a timed challenge:
> "For the next 5 minutes — list 8 ideas. They don't need to be good. They don't need to be realistic. They just need to be *different*. Go."

After they finish:
> "Of those 8 — which one surprised you most? The one you almost didn't say?"

Why it works: Speed constraint forces the brain past the inner critic. The goal is fluency, not quality — quality comes in the converge phase. The "most surprising" question surfaces the idea the user almost dismissed.

---

## Regret Minimization

**When**: User faces a major personal or career decision and is paralyzed by short-term fear (failure, judgment, sunk cost). Especially effective for irreversible decisions.

> "Imagine you're 80 years old, looking back at this decision. Would you regret *doing* it more — or *not doing* it?"

Follow up:
> "The fear you're feeling right now — do you think it will still feel this significant when you look back from 20 years out?"

Why it works: Shifts the frame from short-term risk (fear, embarrassment, failure) to long-term regret — a more stable signal for big decisions. Jeff Bezos used this to leave a well-paying job and start Amazon.

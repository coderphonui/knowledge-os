## 1. ICP — Ideal Customer Profile {#icp}

**When to use:** The user doesn't know who to target, is targeting "everyone", or acquisition feels random.

**Purpose:** Define the specific type of customer who (a) has the problem acutely, (b) is able to pay, (c) is reachable, and (d) gets maximum value fastest.

**Key dimensions:**
- **Firmographic** (B2B): Industry, company size, funding stage, tech stack
- **Demographic** (B2C): Age, income, location, life stage
- **Psychographic:** Values, beliefs, how they see themselves
- **Behavioral:** How they currently solve the problem, where they spend time, how they make buying decisions
- **Trigger event:** What just happened in their life/business that makes them urgently need this NOW?

**Application questions:**
1. Who got the most value from the product so far? What do they have in common?
2. Who referred others? Who churned? What's different?
3. Who had the shortest time-to-value?
4. Who was least price-sensitive?

**Output:** A single paragraph describing one specific person, not a demographic range.
> Example: "Series A B2B SaaS founders (10–50 employees) who just hired their first sales rep and realized their CRM is a mess. They're non-technical, time-starved, and will pay immediately if the ROI is obvious within the first call."

**Common mistake:** Defining ICP too broadly ("SMBs in tech") instead of specifically enough to know exactly where to find them.

## 2. JTBD — Jobs-to-be-Done {#jtbd}

**When to use:** The user doesn't understand why customers actually buy (vs. what features they like), or messaging isn't resonating.

**Purpose:** Understand the deeper motivation behind the purchase. People don't buy products — they "hire" them to do a job in their life.

**Three layers:**
- **Functional job:** The practical task ("I need to send invoices faster")
- **Emotional job:** How they want to feel ("I want to feel like a real business owner")
- **Social job:** How they want to be perceived ("I want clients to see me as professional")

**The "hiring" interview questions:**
1. Walk me through the moment you decided to buy / try this.
2. What were you doing before that led up to that decision?
3. What else did you consider? Why didn't you choose those?
4. What would you have done if this product didn't exist?
5. What almost stopped you from buying?

**Application:** Use the language customers use to describe their job in all marketing copy. Match messaging to the emotional + functional job, not features.

**Example:**
- Feature: "AI-powered meeting transcription"
- Functional job: "I need to remember what was discussed without taking notes"
- Emotional job: "I want to be fully present in conversations instead of scrambling to write"
- Marketing message: "Stop taking notes. Start being present." (targets the emotional job)

## 3. Positioning (April Dunford) {#positioning}

**When to use:** In a crowded market, customers don't understand why to choose this product, or messaging is generic.

**Purpose:** Make the product obviously right for the right customers by being explicitly better than the alternative they're currently using — not compared to direct competitors.

**The Positioning Canvas (5 components):**

1. **Competitive alternatives:** What do customers do if your product doesn't exist? (Often: spreadsheets, manual process, doing nothing — not the direct competitor)
2. **Unique attributes:** What features do you have that alternatives don't?
3. **Value:** What value does each unique attribute deliver?
4. **Target customer:** Who cares intensely about that value?
5. **Market category:** What frame puts you in the best light?

**The positioning statement:**
> For [target customer] who [has this problem], [product] is a [market category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [competitive alternative], [product] [differentiator].

**Common mistakes:**
- Positioning against direct competitors instead of the status quo alternative
- Claiming a benefit without linking it to a unique attribute
- Choosing a market category that invites the wrong comparisons

**Key insight:** The market category you choose sets customer expectations before they've seen a word of copy. "CRM for freelancers" invites comparison to Salesforce. "Client tracker" invites no comparison — it's in its own category.

## 4. Bullseye Framework — Channel Selection {#bullseye}

**When to use:** The user doesn't know which channel to invest in, or is spreading effort across too many channels.

**Purpose:** Systematically identify the one acquisition channel with the highest potential and focus 100% there.

**19 Traction Channels** (from "Traction" by Weinberg & Mares):
Viral Marketing, PR, Unconventional PR, SEM, Social Ads, Offline Ads, SEO, Content Marketing, Email Marketing, Engineering as Marketing, Targeting Blogs, Business Development, Sales, Affiliate Programs, Existing Platforms, Trade Shows, Offline Events, Speaking Engagements, Community Building

**The Bullseye Process:**

**Step 1 — Brainstorm outer ring:** For each of the 19 channels, ask: Could this channel work at all? What would success look like?

**Step 2 — Rank middle ring:** Pick the 3–5 most promising. For each, ask:
- How many customers could I realistically reach?
- What would it cost to acquire one customer?
- How long to get signal (data on whether it works)?

**Step 3 — Test inner ring:** Run cheap experiments on the top 1–3 channels. $500–$2000 max per test. Measure: cost per acquisition, conversion rate, quality of customers.

**Step 4 — Focus:** One channel wins. Go all-in.

**Channel-stage fit:**
- Pre-launch → Content, cold outreach, communities, referrals
- Early traction → Channels that worked in tests, double down
- Scale → Paid acquisition, SEO, partnerships

**Key insight:** At any stage, one channel drives ~70% of growth. Finding it is the job.

## 5. AARRR — Funnel Diagnosis {#aarrr}

**When to use:** The user has some users/customers but isn't growing, or doesn't know where the marketing is breaking down.

**Purpose:** Diagnose exactly where in the funnel the business is leaking, then fix only that layer.

**The 5 layers:**

| Layer | Question | Key Metric |
|-------|----------|-----------|
| **Acquisition** | How do people find you? | CAC, channel conversion rate |
| **Activation** | Do new users have a good first experience? | Time-to-value, onboarding completion |
| **Retention** | Do users come back? | DAU/MAU, churn rate, NPS |
| **Referral** | Do users tell others? | Viral coefficient (k-factor), NPS promoters |
| **Revenue** | Do users pay? | MRR, ARPU, LTV |

**Diagnosis process:**
1. Map current conversion rates at each step
2. Find the biggest drop-off — that's the constraint
3. Fix retention before acquisition (leaky bucket problem)
4. Fix activation before retention

**Common diagnosis patterns:**
- High acquisition, low activation → Onboarding problem or wrong ICP
- High activation, low retention → Product doesn't deliver ongoing value
- High retention, low referral → No trigger for sharing, no referral mechanism
- High retention, low revenue → Pricing/packaging problem

**Key insight:** Fixing acquisition when retention is broken just pours water into a leaky bucket. Always fix downstream layers first.

## 6. PLG vs SLG — GTM Model Decision {#plg-slg}

**When to use:** B2B product deciding how to go to market — should the product sell itself (PLG) or do you need a sales team (SLG)?

**Product-Led Growth (PLG):**
- Product is the primary acquisition and conversion mechanism
- Users try before buying, value is felt before payment
- Examples: Slack, Figma, Dropbox, Notion
- **Works when:** Viral within companies, individual-to-team expansion, low ACV ($0–$500/yr), easy to activate without hand-holding
- **Signals to go PLG:** Users can get value in <10 min, shareability built into product, bottom-up buying behavior in target market

**Sales-Led Growth (SLG):**
- Sales team drives acquisition and conversion
- High-touch demos, negotiations, procurement
- Examples: Salesforce, Workday, enterprise security
- **Works when:** High ACV ($10K+/yr), complex buying process (multiple stakeholders), customization needed, regulated industries
- **Signals to go SLG:** Champion needs to sell internally, ROI requires proof of concept, security/compliance review needed

**Hybrid (Product-Led Sales):**
- PLG drives awareness and activation → SLG closes expansion and enterprise
- Examples: HubSpot, Datadog, MongoDB
- **Works when:** Product has natural freemium tier, team expansion unlocks enterprise deals

**Decision framework:**
1. What is the ACV? (<$1K → PLG, >$25K → SLG, in between → test both)
2. Who is the buyer? (Individual → PLG, committee → SLG)
3. Can the product demonstrate value before payment? (Yes → PLG, No → SLG)
4. How long is the sales cycle? (<1 week → PLG, >1 month → SLG)

## 7. Category Design {#category-design}

**When to use:** The product is genuinely new (no obvious existing category), or existing category comparisons hurt the product (wrong expectations).

**Purpose:** Rather than fighting for position within an existing category, create a new category that the product defines and leads.

**Core principle:** Category leaders capture ~76% of category value (economics of winner-take-most). Owning a category beats competing in one.

**When to consider category design:**
- Existing categories don't fit the product well
- Direct comparisons to incumbents make the product look weak
- The product solves a problem customers didn't know they had
- The problem has a new name that customers don't yet use

**The 3-step process:**

1. **Name the problem** (not the solution): Give the problem a memorable name customers haven't used before. This makes you the de facto authority on the problem.
   > Example: Salesforce didn't sell "better CRM." They named "CRM that lives in the cloud" → "SaaS" → the cloud category.

2. **Define the category:** Write the "Category POV" — a manifesto explaining why the old way of solving the problem is broken and what the new way requires.

3. **Educate the market:** Become the publisher of ideas about the problem, not just the product. Be the person who defines how the industry thinks about it.

**Caution:** Category design requires significant content investment and time (18–36 months). Only worth it if the product is genuinely differentiated. Don't create a category just because you want to avoid competition.

## 8. PMF — Product-Market Fit Diagnosis {#pmf}

**When to use:** User suspects they haven't found PMF yet, or isn't sure why growth is stalling.

**Purpose:** Determine honestly whether PMF exists before investing in marketing. Marketing cannot fix a product that doesn't have PMF.

**The Sean Ellis Test:**
Survey users who have used the product at least twice:
> "How would you feel if you could no longer use [product]?"
- Very disappointed
- Somewhat disappointed
- Not disappointed

**Interpretation:** >40% "very disappointed" = likely PMF. Below that = work on product, not marketing.

**Retention curve test:**
- Plot cohort retention over time (week 1, 2, 4, 8, 12)
- If the curve flattens above 0%, some users find it valuable → PMF exists for a segment
- If the curve approaches 0%, no segment has PMF yet

**5 signals of PMF:**
1. Users come back without prompting
2. Word-of-mouth happens organically (you didn't create it)
3. Users are upset when you take features away
4. Customers use the product for jobs you didn't design for
5. Demand grows faster than you can handle

**If PMF is absent:** Stop marketing investment. Run customer discovery interviews (10–20 interviews with churned users and retained users). Find what the retained users have in common. Narrow the ICP to that segment.

**Key insight:** Marketing amplifies what's already working. If there's no PMF, more marketing = more wasted money.

## 9. Distribution Strategy — Physical Products {#distribution}

**When to use:** Selling a physical product and deciding how to reach customers (D2C, retail, wholesale, marketplace).

**Purpose:** Choose the right distribution channel mix based on margin, control, and scalability goals.

### Channel Options & Trade-offs

| Channel | Margin | Control | Speed to market | Best for |
|---------|--------|---------|----------------|----------|
| **D2C (own website)** | High (60–80%) | Full | Slow | Brand building, premium products, repeat purchase |
| **Marketplace (Shopee, Lazada, Amazon)** | Medium (40–60%) | Low | Fast | Volume, discovery, validation |
| **Retail / Modern trade** | Low (30–50%) | Very low | Slow | Scale, brand awareness, impulse buy |
| **Wholesale / Distributor** | Very low (20–40%) | None | Medium | Geographic expansion without sales team |
| **Social commerce (TikTok Shop, Facebook)** | Medium | Medium | Fast | Impulse, visual products, younger audiences |

### Distribution Strategy by Stage

**Validation stage (0 → first sales):**
- Sell D2C first — maximum feedback, no intermediary dilution
- Use marketplace as discovery channel only
- Avoid retail until product-market fit is proven

**Early growth (product validated):**
- D2C + 1 marketplace (whichever your ICP uses most)
- Build repeat purchase mechanism (subscription, loyalty, bundle)
- Track channel profitability separately — not all channels are equal

**Scale:**
- Expand distribution channels based on unit economics
- Negotiate retail when you have proven velocity data to show
- Protect D2C margin as brand grows

### The Channel Profitability Formula
```
Channel profit = Revenue - COGS - Fulfillment - Channel fee - Marketing cost - Return rate cost
```
Never compare channels by revenue alone. Always compare by profit per order.

### Key Decisions
1. **Where does my ICP actually shop?** → Start there, not everywhere
2. **What is my repurchase rate?** → High repeat = invest in D2C (LTV). Low repeat = marketplace for volume
3. **Is discovery the bottleneck or conversion?** → Discovery = marketplace/social. Conversion = optimize D2C experience
4. **Can I afford the margin on retail?** → Calculate fully-loaded unit economics before pitching retailers

## 10. Trust Ladder — Healthcare & Regulated Products {#trust-ladder}

**When to use:** Selling healthcare products, supplements, medical devices, or any product where claims require credibility and trust is the primary buying barrier.

**Purpose:** Healthcare purchases are uniquely trust-dependent. Customers won't buy if they don't believe it works AND is safe. Marketing must build trust before it can drive purchase.

### The Trust Ladder (low → high credibility)

```
Level 1: Brand promise ("clinically inspired formula")
Level 2: Transparent ingredients / ingredients with known research
Level 3: User testimonials + before/after evidence
Level 4: Third-party testing (lab results, certifications)
Level 5: Clinical studies / published research
Level 6: KOL endorsement (doctor, specialist, pharmacist)
Level 7: Medical/institutional partnership
```

Most products start at Level 1–2. To command premium price and word-of-mouth, aim for Level 4+.

### Healthcare Buyer Types (very different marketing needed)

**End consumer (self-pay, B2C):** Driven by hope, fear, and social proof. Responds to: transformation stories, "I was like you" testimonials, before/after, influencer validation.

**Healthcare provider (doctor, pharmacist):** Driven by clinical evidence and professional reputation. Never sells on hope. Responds to: clinical data, mechanism of action, peer recommendations, samples.

**Institutional buyer (hospital, clinic chain):** Driven by cost, outcomes, compliance. Responds to: health economics (cost vs. outcome), case studies with data, procurement process.

**Key insight:** If doctors/pharmacists recommend your product → consumers buy without question. B2B2C is often more powerful than direct B2C for healthcare.

### The Healthcare Marketing Constraint
- Do NOT make unsubstantiated claims. "Cures X" = regulatory risk. "Supports X" = safer.
- Frame as "evidence-informed" not "proven to cure"
- Let customer testimonials do the strong claim work — user-generated content carries less regulatory risk
- Always have a credentialed voice (doctor, pharmacist) in the communication funnel

### Channel Strategy for Healthcare

**High-trust channels (build these first):**
- KOL / doctor endorsement (HCP-to-consumer funnel)
- Pharmacist recommendation (most trusted product recommender in VN/SEA)
- Community: patient groups, health communities, new-parent groups

**Reach channels (scale after trust is built):**
- Content marketing: "how to solve X problem" → product as solution
- Video content: transformation stories, expert explanation
- Search (Google, Shopee): for people already in buying mode

**Channels to avoid early:**
- Mass advertising without trust foundation = expensive skepticism
- Influencers with no health credibility = backfires for medical claims

## 11. Unit Economics — Pricing & Margin Strategy {#unit-economics}

**When to use:** Product pricing doesn't feel right, margins are unclear, or the business isn't profitable despite decent revenue.

**Purpose:** Understand the financial reality of each customer acquisition and what it means for pricing and channel decisions.

### Key Metrics

**CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost):**
```
CAC = Total marketing + sales spend / Number of new customers acquired
```
Calculate by channel. A customer from Facebook Ads costs differently than one from referral.

**LTV (Customer Lifetime Value):**
```
LTV = Average order value × Purchase frequency × Customer lifespan
```
Or for subscription: `LTV = MRR per customer / Monthly churn rate`

**The LTV:CAC Ratio (health check):**
- `< 1:1` = Losing money on every customer. Broken business model.
- `1:1 – 3:1` = Marginal. Tight margins, limited room for investment.
- `3:1 – 5:1` = Healthy. Can invest in growth.
- `> 5:1` = Strong. Likely under-investing in marketing.

**Payback Period:** How many months to recover CAC from gross margin.
- Bootstrapped: target < 6 months
- VC-backed: target < 12–18 months

### Pricing Strategy

**Cost-plus pricing (avoid as primary strategy):** Price = COGS × markup. Ignores market willingness-to-pay.

**Value-based pricing (best for B2B and premium products):**
- Price based on value delivered, not cost to produce
- Anchor on the cost of the alternative (manual process, competitor, status quo)
- Rule of thumb: capture 10–20% of value delivered for the customer

**Competitive pricing (necessary for commoditized markets):**
- Price within the acceptable range of the category
- Differentiate on value, not price — price wars destroy margins

**Price anchoring:**
- Show a higher tier first to make the target tier feel reasonable
- Annual vs. monthly pricing: annual reduces churn and improves LTV (offer 10–20% discount)

### The "Price Too Low" Problem
Underpricing is a common startup mistake. Signals:
- Customers rarely ask about price
- Churn rate is high (cheap customers are low-commitment)
- You attract customers who negotiate hardest
- Revenue is insufficient to invest in quality/support

Fix: Raise prices 20–30%, accept losing some customers, observe if quality of remaining customers improves.

## 12. Brand vs Performance Marketing {#brand-vs-performance}

**When to use:** Deciding how to allocate marketing budget between brand-building activities and direct-response / paid acquisition.

**Purpose:** These are fundamentally different activities with different time horizons. Confusing them leads to poor decisions.

### Definitions

**Performance marketing:** Measurable, directly attributable to revenue. Paid ads, SEO, email campaigns. ROI visible in days–weeks.

**Brand marketing:** Builds awareness, trust, and preference over time. Content, PR, community, partnerships. ROI visible in months–years.

### The 60/40 Rule (Binet & Field research)
For mature businesses: 60% brand, 40% performance delivers best long-term results.
For early-stage: flip this — 80% performance (learn what works), 20% brand.

### When to Invest in Each

**Invest in performance first when:**
- Product-market fit is not yet confirmed
- Budget is limited (need fast feedback loops)
- Need to understand CAC and which channels work
- Pre-revenue or early revenue

**Shift toward brand when:**
- CAC is rising on performance channels (saturation)
- Product has PMF and retention is strong
- You want to command premium price
- Direct competitors are commoditizing the category

### Practical Rule of Thumb
Early stage: every marketing dollar should either (a) bring a measurable customer or (b) build an asset that will bring customers later (content, community, reputation). Pure brand spend without a measurable pathway is a luxury for later.

### Brand Building for Non-Marketers
Brand = what people say about you when you're not in the room. Build it by:
1. Delivering a product experience worth talking about (word of mouth)
2. Taking a visible position on something the ICP cares about
3. Consistently showing up in the channels where the ICP spends time
4. Creating content/ideas that the ICP wants to share
